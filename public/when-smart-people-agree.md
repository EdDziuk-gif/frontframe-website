One of the dangers of listening to smart people is that they are often right.

Bill Ackman recently observed that the likelihood of disruption has increased enormously. He's probably right. The same tools that make it easier to build a company make it easier to challenge one. Computing power is available to almost anyone. Capital is more accessible than it has ever been.

Most people hear that observation and immediately begin looking for threats. Which competitors should we fear? Which technologies should we monitor? Which industries are vulnerable?

What interests me is something else.

Every major technological shift destroys advantages. The internet changed information. AI is changing the economics of knowledge work. Looking backward, the pattern is obvious: whenever one advantage disappears, another quietly takes its place.

When information became abundant, information itself became less valuable. The advantage moved to judgment. When websites became easy to build, websites ceased to be the advantage. The advantage moved to understanding customers.

When everyone gains access to the same tools, the advantage is somewhere else.

Businesses built around access lose their position when access becomes easy. Businesses built around technology lose their advantage when everyone has it.

The businesses that will thrive are not the ones defending yesterday's advantages. They are the ones competing on things that are harder to disrupt.

The accountant whose clients call before making important decisions. The contractor whose customers wait months rather than hire someone else. The physician whose patients recommend her without being asked. The business owner who understands a market so thoroughly that competitors struggle to explain why customers remain loyal.

None of these people are immune to disruption. They have simply built around things that are harder to disrupt: trust, judgment, deep understanding, earned reputation.

These things operate on a different timetable. They cannot be rushed, rented, or downloaded. They must be earned the slow way.

That is why I keep coming back to the same thought: the future belongs to the businesses that stop competing on things everyone can build.
