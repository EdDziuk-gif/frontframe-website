How I Became a Systems Architect in 10,000 Easy Steps

A career spent solving problems that systems should have solved first.

FrontFrame did not emerge from a sudden business idea. It emerged from decades of encountering the same operational fragility in different forms — businesses that worked, until they didn't, because too much lived inside people instead of systems. This is the story of how I came to understand that.

The First Problem: Where Risk Accumulates

I joined the audit department at Union Pacific in 1981 — not because the large-firm track appealed to me, but because the work itself did. What I discovered quickly was that the work had a problem.

Extended audits in St. Louis, Fort Worth, and Long Beach often ran three months at a time. I was the only auditor carrying my own computer. I built Lotus 1-2-3 spreadsheets to automate staff expense reporting, optimize meal reimbursements, and run regression analyses — not because anyone asked me to, but because the manual approach consumed time that should have been going elsewhere.

I was less interested in procedure than in improving the system around the work. I didn't have a name for that yet.

The Second Problem: Operational Fragility

At the Phoenix transit system, I worked on two things that stayed with me. One was a media tracking and management system. The other was a structural argument against part-time driver programs — not on policy grounds, but on operational ones. Part-time arrangements introduced fragility that the system couldn't absorb.

Once the structural problems were solved, I was ready to move on. That pattern — solve the problem, lose interest in the job — would repeat itself.

An Interruption: Systems Alone Are Not Enough

For a few years I attempted to build a business through Nikken Technologies. The system wasn't the problem. I was. The model required me to be something I wasn't, and I didn't recognize that until I had already invested considerable time finding out.

Systems alone are not enough.

Eventually I obtained an Arizona CPA license and returned to accounting.

The Third Problem: Time Consumed by Weak Processes

Public accounting fit me better than I expected. I could prepare a tax return in about an hour. The paper systems of the day often required three or four additional hours just to process and deliver the work.

That inefficiency bothered me constantly. Not as an inconvenience — as a structural problem worth solving.

The best thing that happened to me in those years was John Matson. He was a perfectionist and the ideal mentor. He never removed work from my desk, but he would never accept anything less than camera-ready perfection. I routinely worked more hours than I billed. Under his discipline I learned that standards and efficiency are not in conflict — weak systems create the appearance of a tradeoff that doesn't actually exist.

I remained in that environment for eighteen years.

The Fourth Problem: The Gap Between Analysis and Distribution

Alongside my accounting work, I subscribed to the AAII financial database in 2002 and built my own database containing five years of trailing financial data for every publicly listed U.S. company. I paired it with an Excel model that could retrieve company data and generate a Plimsoll-style financial analysis in about a minute.

The system worked.

The problem was distribution. I had no mechanism to turn the analysis into anything someone else could act on. The insight existed inside a spreadsheet on my computer and nowhere else. I identified companies I believed were strong short-sale candidates and eventually recognized that the risks and distractions were beginning to interfere with my professional work.

I stepped back. But the problem stayed with me: a working system is not the same thing as a working business.

The Fifth Problem: Value Trapped Inside People

The next chapter began with Eleanor's honey business.

I convinced her that the business could never reach its full value operating from our garage. After a difficult search, she found a small location in a business park. Within five years she had transformed it into a thriving retail operation — her instincts for retail space shaped over twenty-four years in her family's jewelry store.

Eventually we sold the business with the help of an excellent broker.

Only afterward did we understand what we were actually looking at. There was no clear picture of who the customers were. No reliable way to contact them individually. No SOPs. Recipes existed largely in memory. The operation depended on a highly capable general manager whose knowledge lived entirely with her.

The business was successful. But much of its value still lived inside people instead of systems.

That realization stayed with us.

The Sixth Problem: Knowing and Doing Are Not the Same Thing

Before FrontFrame, I spent time training as an Emyth business coach. The systematization framework made complete sense to me — it addressed exactly the problem I had been circling for years. I committed to it fully.

What I discovered was that the average coaching engagement lasted about six weeks. Not because the framework was wrong. Because clients could absorb the concepts and still be unable to implement them. Knowing what needs to be done and knowing how to do it right now are different problems. The coaching model assumed that closing the first gap would close the second. It didn't.

I wasn't able to make that model work either. It required me to be someone I wasn't — again.

But the observation stayed. If implementation is the real problem, the solution isn't better explanation. It's a structure that reduces the work to the smallest thing a person can actually do today.

That became a design constraint for FrontFrame.

The Response: Designing Transferability from the Beginning

The next venture would begin with the end in mind.

That became the foundation for Elie's Bird Sitting. This time, the business was designed from the beginning to operate as a system rather than as a personality. The system handles scheduling, communication, and routine customer questions even when Eleanor is unavailable.

It is a small business by design. The point was never scale. The point was that it would work without depending on her constant presence — and that its value would be legible to someone who had never met her.

The Pattern

Looking back, the pattern is obvious.

Throughout my career, I was rarely drawn to titles, institutions, or hierarchy. I was drawn to systems that were inefficient, fragile, undocumented, or overly dependent on individuals. Again and again I found myself trying to make them clearer, more resilient, and easier for other people to operate.

It took me a long time to realize this was not simply part of the work.

It was the work.

Most of the businesses I encountered throughout that career worked. They generated revenue, served customers, and kept their owners occupied. What they rarely did was operate independently of the people who built them.

The knowledge was real. The capability was real. But it lived in memory, in habit, in the presence of specific individuals — and nowhere else.

FrontFrame is the practical expression of that observation. Not a solution looking for a problem. A response to the same problem, encountered again and again, across four decades.

If your business depends heavily on memory, improvisation, or constant owner involvement — if it could not operate, even briefly, without you — we should probably talk.
